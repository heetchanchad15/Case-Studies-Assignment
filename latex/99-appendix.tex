\subsection{Core Modelling Code}

Listing~\ref{lst:models} shows the two estimators and the feature pipeline. The
vectoriser sits \emph{inside} the pipeline so that
\texttt{StratifiedKFold} refits it on each training fold; fitting it once over the whole
corpus would leak test-fold vocabulary and inflate every metric, the pitfall
\citet{pageEvaluating} raises on slide~35.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Feature pipeline and the two estimators
(\texttt{code/03\_analysis.py}).}, label={lst:models}]
def make_vectoriser():
    # Fitted inside every fold, never on the full corpus.
    return TfidfVectorizer(
        sublinear_tf=True, min_df=3, max_features=20000,
        ngram_range=(1, 2), strip_accents="unicode", lowercase=True,
    )

def make_models():
    return {
        "Linear SVM": Pipeline([
            ("tfidf", make_vectoriser()),
            ("clf", LinearSVC(C=1.0, class_weight="balanced",
                              max_iter=5000, random_state=SEED)),
        ]),
        "Neural network (MLP)": Pipeline([
            ("tfidf", make_vectoriser()),
            ("clf", MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(64,), activation="relu",
                                  solver="adam", alpha=1e-4, batch_size=256,
                                  learning_rate_init=1e-3, max_iter=30,
                                  early_stopping=True, n_iter_no_change=3,
                                  validation_fraction=0.1, random_state=SEED)),
        ]),
    }

def precision_at_capacity(y_true, score, capacity=0.01):
    """Precision over the top-scoring slice a moderation queue can review."""
    k = max(1, int(round(len(y_true) * capacity)))
    top = np.argsort(score)[::-1][:k]
    return float(y_true[top].mean()), k
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Issues Encountered and How They Were Addressed}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The first advertisement selected had closed.} The 2026-start version of
    this role expired before submission. Rather than cite a dead link, the employer's job
    index was searched for the live equivalent; the 2027-start listing was confirmed open
    (an active \emph{Apply} control, closing 30 August 2026) and substituted. The text of
    the two postings is materially identical.

    \item \textbf{The obvious toxicity dataset was behind a login.} The widely used Jigsaw
    release is distributed through Kaggle, which requires an account and so fails the
    specification's requirement that the link be accessible. The original Wulczyn et al.
    release on Figshare was used instead: same underlying annotations, CC0 licence, no
    account.

    \item \textbf{Encoded whitespace polluted the features.} Wikipedia comments store line
    and tab breaks as the literal strings \texttt{NEWLINE\_TOKEN} and \texttt{TAB\_TOKEN}.
    Left in place these become two of the highest-frequency terms in the vocabulary and
    carry no signal. They are stripped before vectorising.

    \item \textbf{The two estimators expose different score interfaces.}
    \texttt{LinearSVC} provides \texttt{decision\_function} but not
    \texttt{predict\_proba}. Because both ROC and precision-recall curves depend only on
    the \emph{ranking} induced by the score, the raw decision values are used directly;
    no calibration step is needed for the curves, though it would be needed before
    interpreting a score as a probability in production.

    \item \textbf{Neural network runtime.} A dense hidden layer over a full-vocabulary
    sparse matrix was too slow to cross-validate on the larger corpus. The vocabulary was
    capped at 20,000 features and the network reduced to a single 64-unit hidden layer
    with early stopping. Both models share the identical vectoriser configuration so the
    comparison stays fair.

    \item \textbf{Class imbalance made the default threshold useless for the SVM.} At the
    default decision boundary the minority class was barely predicted at all.
    \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} was enabled, which is why the SVM's operating point
    favours recall over precision --- a choice revisited in
    Section~\ref{sec:Data Analysis}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Use of Generative AI}

Claude (Anthropic) was used throughout, in the manner declared on
page~\pageref{sec:Data Analysis}. Representative prompts:

\begin{itemize}
    \item ``Find live machine learning engineer job advertisements in Australia and verify
    the listing URLs actually resolve.''
    \item ``Propose two publicly available datasets relevant to content-moderation triage
    that download without an account, and confirm the download URLs respond.''
    \item ``Write a stratified cross-validation harness comparing a linear SVM and an MLP,
    fitting the vectoriser inside each fold, and report precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC and
    PR-AUC plus a paired $t$-test across folds.''
    \item ``Condense this paragraph to at most 50 words without losing the citation.''
\end{itemize}

Two defects survived into a compiled draft and were caught only by rendering the PDF and
inspecting every page rather than trusting the source. The typeset job advertisement
originally ran to two pages, of which Figure~\ref{fig:job advertisement} included only the
first --- the preferred qualifications were silently missing. Separately, the cover letter
inherited the ACM template's one-and-a-half line spacing and broke across two pages,
defeating the purpose of a stand-alone letter. Both are corrected in this version. The
lesson generalises to the analysis itself: generated output looks finished long before it
is correct, and the only reliable check is to inspect the artefact rather than the code
that produced it.

feed-forward neural network under stratified five-fold cross validation.

\vspace{8pt}

\noindent Two findings from that work speak directly to your team's problem. First, I
evaluated the models the way a moderation queue actually runs. Because only 5.8\% and
9.6\% of the two corpora are removal-worthy, accuracy is close to meaningless, so I
reported precision, recall and area under the precision-recall curve, and measured
precision across the top 1\% of the ranked stream --- the slice a fixed review team could
genuinely process in a day. Second, I tested whether a model survives a change of source.
A classifier scoring 0.96 ROC-AUC on its own corpus flagged 73\% of the other platform's
traffic against a true base rate of 5.8\%, because the two label schemes disagree about
whether offensive language is the same thing as hate speech. That failure is invisible in
single-dataset metrics and would surface in production as mass wrongful removals of
ordinary speech.

\vspace{8pt}

\noindent I also treat fairness as part of the engineering rather than a review step after
it. Published work shows hate-speech classifiers systematically over-flag African-American
English, and the Wikipedia corpus ships annotator demographics precisely because who
labels the data decides what counts as toxic. Before I would ship a classifier, I would
want its error rates broken down by dialect and community, not just its headline score.
